How WebSWR works

WebSWR tests whether a retirement portfolio would have lasted, using real market history (1871–2025) and Monte Carlo simulation. Everything runs in your browser — no account, no tracking.

The basics

  1. Enter your portfolio value, how many years it must last, and your asset mix (must total 100%).
  2. Pick a spending strategy and amount.
  3. Click Run simulation.

The big number is your success rate — the share of historical retirements (or Monte Carlo trials) in which the money lasted the whole period. Green ≥ 95%, amber ≥ 75%, red below.

Historical vs Monte Carlo

  • Historical replays every actual sequence of returns since 1871 — a 30-year plan is tested starting in 1871, 1872, … 1996.
  • Income and tax. Enter Social Security and pensions at their gross amount, as on your benefit statement. Each income row has a Source: Social Security and pensions are taxed at your effective rate, while Tax-free is for Roth income or an amount you have already netted. Taxing all of Social Security slightly overstates the tax, since at most 85% of it is taxable — the safe side. Under Extra expenses and income, Income is taxed too, and Tax-free is for an inheritance or a gift, which are not federally taxed. Links made before this distinction read an untyped income as taxed: the conservative reading.
  • The Social Security cut. The 2026 Trustees Report projects the retirement trust fund running out in late 2032. Under current law benefits then fall automatically to the 78% that payroll taxes can fund, for everyone, including people already retired and benefits claimed later. That cut is on by default and applied to every Soc. Sec. row from 2032. Congress may well act first, but assuming it will is the optimistic choice, so it is yours to make by unticking the box. The cut year is a calendar year, mapped onto retirement years from the year the plan starts; a shared link keeps its own start year, so it reproduces the same plan when opened later.
  • Planning for two. A couple's money has to last until the second death, which is much later than either life expectancy alone: for two 65-year-olds the published SSA table gives about a 51% chance at least one reaches 90, against 24% for a man alone. Tick Plan for two and the mortality chart uses the chance that at least one of you is alive. The two lifetimes are treated as independent; in reality spouses' mortality is correlated, which makes it likelier you both die earlier — so this, too, errs toward assuming the money must last longer.
  • Year by year. The table under Details replays one historical retirement in full — start balance, spending, income, expenses, tax, withdrawal, fees, the year's return and inflation, and the end balance — computed by exactly the same code as the historical backtest, so every row matches it to the cent (Monte Carlo paths are synthetic, so they have no year-by-year history to show). It opens on the worst case. Every year the portfolio covers in full satisfies spending + expenses + tax = income + withdrawal, so you can check it yourself. The exception is a year in which the portfolio is used up: its needs could not be met in full. That is a failure if it happens before the final year, but in the final year it completes the plan, which is how VPW is designed to end. Start years marked partial are too recent to finish: the table stops where history does, with money still left. Download CSV saves it with both today's and nominal dollars.
  • Comparing retirement lengths is exact for constant-dollar, percentage-of-portfolio and CAPE plans, whose yearly draw depends only on the balance. It is approximate for VPW and Guyton-Klinger, which size the draw from the years remaining — so changing the length changes the spending rule itself, not just the stretch of history being tested. A longer plan draws less each year, so a start year that ran dry under a shorter plan can still be solvent when the data runs out, at which point its outcome is unknown rather than failed and it leaves the success rate entirely. Measured across every allocation and length, that can let a longer plan score up to about 1.8 points higher. It is a limit of the historical record, not a bug to be engineered away: compare these two strategies at a single length.
  • Return adjustments shift an asset class's return by a fixed amount every year — a haircut when negative. The offset is added to each year's return, so −0.25%/yr lowers the nominal return by exactly 0.25 points, and the real return by a/(1+inflation) — about 0.24 points at 3% inflation, since the engine deflates geometrically rather than subtracting; it is not compounded or scaled by horizon, because a constant offset is what “0.25% less every year” means, and anything cleverer would be a model of our own sitting on top of the historical record.
    The usual reason to use it is that history may be too generous: high starting valuations argue for lower future equity returns, and today's bond yields may differ from the historical average. A common sensitivity is stocks −0.25%/yr with bonds +0.25%/yr. An offset on an asset you hold none of changes nothing, and the results say so rather than claiming a stress test that never ran; the exception is Cash, which also sets the rate on the monthly withdrawal bucket because that money is held in the same T-bills. Be aware it cuts both ways — raising returns makes any plan look safer, so results with an adjustment active are labelled on screen and in the printed report, and they are not the historical record.
  • Monte Carlo builds thousands of synthetic futures: Bootstrap draws random past years; Block bootstrap (variable streaks) — the default — draws runs of consecutive years, redrawing each run's length between your Shortest and Longest settings; Block bootstrap (fixed streaks) uses one length for every run; Parametric samples a normal distribution fit to history.
    Streak length matters more than it looks, and in the opposite direction to most people's guess. Real markets tend to mean-revert — bad years are often followed by recovery — so chopping history into single years destroys that recovery and invents long slumps that never happened. Drawing single years is therefore harsher than the historical record itself (about 93% success on a 4% plan, against 97.6% historically), while longer streaks keep more real sequence and land closer to history (12–20 year runs give about 96%). The 2–8 default sits in between deliberately. Bootstrap remains the toughest test; the block methods are the more realistic ones.

A caveat on the success rate. Historical cycles overlap — a 30-year window starting in 1965 shares 29 of its years with the 1966 one — so 126 cycles reflect only a handful of truly independent eras, and the failures cluster around a few bad episodes (1906, 1929, the late-1960s stagflation). Treat the number as a stress-test against everything markets have actually done, not as a probability with decimal-point precision.

Every retirement starts in January. The historical data is annual — one return and one inflation figure per calendar year — so each cycle begins on January 1, and the engine never sees a retirement that began partway through a year, such as one starting at the market’s peak in September 1929. Monthly data adds eleven more starting points a year, and the worst of them can be worse than any January start, so this record can look slightly better than a month-by-month one.

Withdrawal strategies

  • Constant dollar (the “4% rule”) — a fixed amount, raised each year for inflation.
  • Percentage of portfolio — a fixed % of the current balance each year. On its own it only ever spends a fraction of what you still hold, so it shrinks rather than running dry — but your income swings with the market, and a spending floor (or a large fixed expense) can force the draw up until the money really does run out. Its Withdrawal frequency toggle chooses how the year's money is paid out: Annual takes it as one January lump; Monthly parks the year's withdrawal in a risk-free 3-month T-bill account and draws it down monthly, crediting the interest back to the portfolio at year-end (historical T-bill rates; 0% before 1928, when the series begins).
  • CAPE-based (Big ERN) — a valuation-aware percentage: each year you withdraw a + b × (1/CAPE) of the balance, so the rate is lower when stocks are expensive (high CAPE) and higher when they're cheap. See below.
  • Guyton-Klinger guardrails — constant-dollar with rules that trim spending after bad years and raise it after good ones.
  • VPW (variable percentage) — amortizes the portfolio down to ~$0 by the end, like a self-managed pension. See the detailed section below, including how it differs from the Bogleheads VPW.

Real vs Nominal

The balance chart can show today’s dollars (real) or face value (nominal). Real is the honest view — $1M in 50 years buys far less than today.

CAPE-based withdrawals (Big ERN)

The CAPE (cyclically-adjusted P/E, or “Shiller PE”) compares today’s price to the last 10 years of real earnings — a rough gauge of how expensive stocks are. Big ERN’s dynamic rule sets each year’s withdrawal rate to a + b × (1/CAPE), where 1/CAPE is the earnings yield. Defaults a = 1.75% and b = 0.5 (his toolbox settings): at CAPE 30 that’s 1.75% + 0.5×3.33% ≈ 3.4%; at a cheap CAPE 15 it rises to ≈ 5.1%. You spend less when valuations are stretched and more when they’re not, which historically avoids the worst sequence-of-returns busts.

Historical backtests use each year’s actual Shiller CAPE as of the start of that year (January — the value a retiree could really have acted on; validated against multpl.com). Monte Carlo starts from the current CAPE and evolves it forward with the simulated returns. That’s why the “first year” of a historical run is a range — every start year had its own valuation — and won’t equal the today’s-CAPE readout under the inputs, which is what year 1 looks like if you retire now.

The “better CAPE.” The headline CAPE runs permanently high partly because of buybacks and old tax rates. Following ERN’s “better CAPE”, WebSWR’s current CAPE grosses recent earnings up by the retained-earnings yield and normalizes them to today’s corporate tax rate — a lower, fairer number that supports a slightly higher withdrawal rate today. It reproduces ERN’s published adjusted CAPE to within a fraction of a point.

Kept current automatically. Nothing is fetched at runtime (that’s the whole security model). Instead, a daily server-side job re-runs the data pipeline and redeploys, so the shipped CAPE tracks the latest value on its own — there’s nothing to enter or update by hand.

VPW — amortization-based withdrawal

VPW treats your portfolio like a self-managed pension. Each year it withdraws the slice of your current balance that would draw the account down to about $0 over your remaining years, assuming a fixed real growth rate in between. It is the same math as a loan payment, run in reverse: withdrawal % = PMT(assumed real return, years left), taken at the start of the year — so the factor rises every year and the final year withdraws 100%. Because the percentage always applies to what you actually hold, VPW shrinks rather than running dry — though a spending floor or a large fixed expense can still force the draw past what the portfolio holds and exhaust it. The trade-off is that your income swings with the market and is designed to finish near zero, so it pairs best with Social Security or a pension covering your essentials. (Add those under Income and expenses, or set a Spending floor — note a floor high enough to force over-spending in a bad sequence can make even VPW fail.)

The Assumed real return field (default 3.4%) is the only knob. A higher rate front-loads spending — it bets the balance will grow faster, so it draws more early and less later; a lower rate flattens and defers spending. It is a planning assumption for the withdrawal schedule only — the simulation still runs your balance against real market history (or the Monte Carlo draws), so a rosy assumed return does not make the money last, it just spends it sooner.

How this differs from the Bogleheads VPW. The skeleton is identical — both amortize the balance to ~$0 over the years remaining, at the start of each year. The difference is where the rate comes from. WebSWR uses the single number you set, applied whatever your allocation. The Bogleheads spreadsheet (longinvest’s VPW) instead derives the rate from your stock/bond mix — assuming a higher real return for stocks than for bonds (on the order of ~5% and ~2% real in its published tables) and blending them by weight — then ships the result as a fixed percentage table. So for a stock-heavy portfolio the Bogleheads rate is higher than 3.4% and it prescribes larger early withdrawals than WebSWR does out of the box; to line the two up, set this field to your allocation’s blended rate (a 60/40 mix works out to roughly 3.5–4% in its tables). WebSWR also keeps your allocation fixed each year rather than following VPW’s recommended equity glidepath, and computes the percentage live instead of from a lookup table.

Find max spending

Open Find max spending under the Run button, enter a target success rate and click Find max spending for. WebSWR searches for the highest constant spending that still hits that target — against history or Monte Carlo. The answer appears below it and is applied to your plan.

Throughout the form, a small i beside a field opens a short explanation, and each folded section shows a one-line summary of its settings, so nothing that affects your results is ever out of sight.

Will your money outlast you?

Set your age and sex, and the “Rich, broke, or dead?” chart layers SSA life-table survival odds onto your simulation: for each year it shows the chance you’re alive with money, alive but broke, or deceased. Often you’re far more likely to pass away with money left over than to run out of it.

A caveat that runs one way. The SSA table understates how long most people using a tool like this will live, for two reasons. It is a period table — a snapshot that applies today’s death rates at each age to every future year — while real cohorts keep benefiting from medical progress: the 2026 Trustees Report’s cohort table (what actuaries use for exactly this question) expects people turning 65 in 2026 to live 19.2 more years for men and 21.8 for women, against 18.1 and 20.7 in this table. And it is a population average, while life expectancy climbs steeply with income: at age 40, the richest 1% of Americans outlive the poorest 1% by 14.6 years for men and 10.1 for women (Chetty et al., JAMA 2016). Both make the chart overstate your chance of dying and therefore understate longevity risk — they err toward making a plan look safer.

The Longevity setting counters this by reading the table as if you were younger, which actuaries call an age setback. The default of 2 years roughly closes the gap to the cohort table for someone retiring now; it closes less of it for younger people, who have more years of medical progress ahead of them. Choose 4 or 6 years if your income or health is well above average, or as published to see the raw table. Whatever the setting, treat the “deceased” band as a floor, not a forecast.

Loan calculator

The Loan calc tab (top right) amortizes any fixed-rate loan — mortgage, car, student loan. It shows the monthly payment, total interest, payoff time, and how extra monthly principal shortens the loan and cuts interest.

Compound interest

The Compound tab grows a lump sum plus regular additions: pick the rate, how many times per year it compounds, and whether additions land at the start or end of each compounding period (the annual addition is spread evenly across those periods). You get the future value, total contributed vs. interest earned, and a year-by-year breakdown. Values are nominal — inflation and taxes are not applied.

Income and expenses

Add Social Security, pensions, or one-off expenses. Years count from retirement (year 1 = your first year retired). Tick COLA to grow an amount with inflation.

What isn't modelled unless you add it. Healthcare and long-term care: medical prices have long risen faster than general inflation, and a long-term-care stay can cost more in a year than many retirees spend in total, so an Expense row for the later years is worth considering. Your own inflation rate: your spending, and any row with COLA ticked, grows with CPI-U, the index for all urban consumers, and prices for older households can rise faster. The BLS’s experimental CPI-E for Americans 62 and over averaged about 0.2 points a year more than CPI-U from 1982 to 2011, mostly because older households spend more on medical care, though the Congressional Budget Office found it ran lower in the years after 2007. To allow for a higher personal rate, lower every asset’s Return adjustment by the difference: for the portfolio, that has nearly the same effect.

Where the data comes from

Annual total returns: 1928–present from Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern) with CPI-U inflation from the BLS; 1871–1927 reconstructed from Robert Shiller’s data (stocks, bonds, inflation only — an annual approximation). Gold, cash, corporate bonds, real estate and small-cap begin in 1928, so using them limits cycles to 1928+.

Privacy & safety

No accounts, no analytics, and this app sends nothing you enter anywhere — every simulation runs locally in your browser. The hosting CDN, Cloudflare, adds its own bot-detection script for security. That is Cloudflare's code rather than ours, and it runs inside the page like any script would, so we can't vouch for everything it can read — but this app never passes your inputs to it, and it isn't built to collect them. Copy link encodes your scenario into the URL so you can bookmark or share it; that link contains your numbers, so share it deliberately.
